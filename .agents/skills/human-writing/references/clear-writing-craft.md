# Clear writing craft

Writing that avoids AI tells can still be bad. This file is the positive half: how good nonfiction prose gets made. It distills Orwell ("Politics and the English Language", 1946), Strunk and White, Joseph Williams (*Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*), Steven Pinker (*The Sense of Style*), Gopen and Swan ("The Science of Scientific Writing", 1990), William Zinsser (*On Writing Well*), Paul Graham ("Write Simply", 2021), US federal plain-language guidelines, and Werner Robitza's "Writing Simply in the Age of AI" (2026).

## 1. Before writing

- **One reader.** Name them: "Priya, who owns the billing service and has ten minutes." Pinker's "classic style" is the stance of showing a smart reader something you both can see; the enemy is the curse of knowledge, forgetting what the reader does not know. The AI failure is the opposite: writing for a reader who knows nothing, so it explains everything to everyone.
- **One point.** If you cannot say it in two sentences, you are not ready to draft. Graham: fancy writing can conceal a lack of ideas; simple writing makes the absence obvious, including to you.
- **Real material.** List the specifics you actually have: numbers, names, examples, the thing that happened. Good prose is mostly good material. If the list is empty, ask the user before drafting padding.

## 2. Structure

- **Lead with what the reader needs.** In functional writing (emails, replies, docs, reports), the answer, decision, or ask comes first; context after, and only as much as the reader lacks. This is "bottom line up front".
- **Let the content choose the shape.** Some useful arcs (Robitza): context, finding, implication; state before, turning point, state after; scene, reflection, meaning. Use one; do not bolt a preview and a recap onto it.
- **Don't advertise the structure unless the document is long** enough that the reader needs a map (reports, papers). A blog post that says "In this post I'll cover three things" and ends "To recap" has the SlopShape machine shape.
- **Headings name content.** "Why the cache made it slower", not "Overview" or "Key considerations". Sentence case. Fewer than you think.
- **End when the content ends.** The last sentence is the last thing worth saying, often a consequence, a next step, or a concrete fact. Not a summary, not a moral.

## 3. Paragraphs

- One topic per paragraph (Strunk). The first sentence tells a skimming reader what the paragraph is about.
- Each paragraph should connect to the previous one; if you can shuffle paragraphs without the reader noticing, you have a list, not an argument.
- Length follows content. One line is fine when one line is all it takes; four hundred words is fine when the idea needs it.

## 4. Sentences

Williams' principles are the most useful single tool for rewriting stiff prose:

- **Characters as subjects, actions as verbs.** "We decided" not "A decision was made"; "The server fails" not "The server experiences failure"; "we evaluated" not "we conducted an evaluation of". Find the buried verb inside the noun (-tion, -ment, -ance) and let it do the work.
- **Old before new** (Gopen and Swan). Start a sentence with information the reader already has (the topic position) and put the new, important information at the end (the stress position). This is what makes prose flow without transition words.
- **Keep subject and verb close.** Long interruptions between them force the reader to hold their breath.
- **One main idea per sentence** as a default; combine when two ideas are tightly linked. A colon, semicolon, or parenthesis can hold two thoughts together honestly.
- **Positive form over negative** where possible ("He forgot" rather than "He did not remember"), except where the negation is the fact.
- **Active voice** when the actor matters; passive when the actor is unknown or irrelevant or when it keeps old-before-new order.

## 5. Words

Orwell's six rules, still the best short list:
1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

Additions:
- "is" before "serves as"; "has" before "boasts"; "use" before "utilize/leverage"; "about" before "approximately"; "so/because" before "thereby"; "show" before "demonstrate".
- Repeat the right word instead of cycling synonyms. "Said" is invisible.
- Use the jargon your reader uses (a domain term is precise for experts) and nothing fancier.
- Concrete over abstract: a number over "significant", a named tool over "various solutions".
- Hedge only where you are uncertain, and hedge once. Avoid "clearly", "obviously", "undoubtedly" for things you cannot back up.

## 6. Rhythm

Gary Provost's famous demonstration: a run of five-word sentences turns into a drone; varying length makes music. The practical rule is that sentence length should vary because thoughts vary. A long sentence that carries an argument through several turns, then a short one. Do not manufacture rhythm by chopping sentences into fragments; that is the punchy-fragment tell.

Read the draft aloud. Anywhere you run out of breath, split. Anywhere three sentences in a row have the same shape, merge or recast one.

## 7. Editing process

1. **Draft fast, edit slowly.** Graham writes the first draft quickly and spends days cutting.
2. **Cut first.** Most edits are deletions: preambles, restatements, the paragraph that explains what the next paragraph will say, the closing recap. Zinsser's "clutter" is the enemy.
3. **Check each claim** against the source. Add nothing you cannot support; drop nothing that mattered.
4. **Read as the reader.** Where would they stop reading? What would they ask? Answer that, delete the rest.
5. **Read aloud** for rhythm and for sentences no human would say.

## 8. Checklist

- [ ] I know the one reader and the one point.
- [ ] The first paragraph (or line) gives the reader what they need most.
- [ ] Every paragraph has one job; each first sentence tells a skimmer what it is.
- [ ] Characters are subjects, actions are verbs; buried verbs rescued.
- [ ] No sentence restates the previous one; no recap ending.
- [ ] Specific over general everywhere I had material; gaps flagged, not filled.
- [ ] Plain words; the right word repeated rather than cycled.
- [ ] Length varies because the ideas vary; punctuation at human density.
- [ ] Formatting only where the reader scans; headings name content.
- [ ] The piece stops when it is done.
