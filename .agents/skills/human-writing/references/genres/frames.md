# Frames and formats

The frame sets POV, document form, and what the narrator can know. Frames that pretend to be real documents (fiction framed as real) live or die on believability.

## First-person account ("this happened to me")

- One narrator, one consistent voice with a specific life: job, city, age, habits, the way they talk. Not a generic "I".
- The account has a reason to exist: they're asking for help, warning others, confessing, documenting before something happens.
- The narrator knows only what they could know, and misremembers or doubts some of it.
- Casual register: contractions, digressions, real place and brand names, a detail that isn't relevant but is true to life.
- No author's voice breaking frame: no "stay tuned", no "this is a work of fiction", no moral.
- Present-day framing ("This was two years ago. I'm writing it down because...") then past-tense events is the classic shape.

## r/nosleep-style post

Platform rules are in `platforms.md`. Craft specifics:
- **Title** reads like a real Reddit post: plain, specific, often understated ("My neighbor keeps mowing his lawn at 3am", "I work nights at a gas station in Nebraska. There are rules."). Not "The Horror of Maple Street".
- **Opening** in the first two lines: who they are, the situation, the hook. No weather.
- **Believability** comes from mundane competence: how the job works, what the coworker's name is, what they did first (called someone, checked the camera, Googled it).
- **Structure** for series: each part ends on a real development, not a fake-out; updates reference comments naturally only if the user wants that.
- **Ending:** the narrator must be able to have posted it. Unresolved and ongoing is the native ending.

## Rules horror ("rules for working the night shift")

- A list of rules given to the narrator, usually by an employer or relative, presented in a matter-of-fact tone. The horror is in the specific, strange, unexplained rules and what happens when one is broken, missing, or wrong.
- Keep rules concrete and slightly bureaucratic ("Do not restock aisle 4 after 2:10am. If the milk is warm, leave the store."). Avoid rules that are just "don't look at it".
- The turn: a rule contradicts another, a rule has been crossed out, or the narrator learns why a rule exists.

## Epistolary and diary

- Letters, emails, journal entries, texts, forum posts, police reports, each in its own authentic form (date lines, signatures, typos in texts, official language in reports).
- Gaps between documents do the work; the reader infers what happened between entries.
- Each writer sounds different. The document's purpose shapes its voice (a diary is private and unguarded; a report is careful and hides things).
- Dates and times must be consistent across documents.

## Transcripts and interviews

- Speaker labels, [inaudible], [crosstalk], false starts, "um", interruptions. Real transcripts are messy; AI transcripts are suspiciously fluent paragraphs.
- The interviewer's questions reveal as much as the answers.

## Found footage (in prose) and analog horror

Found footage in prose is a log of recordings: timestamps, camera descriptions, what can and can't be seen, audio-only stretches. The implication that the recorder is missing or dead adds stakes.

Analog horror (Local 58, *The Mandela Catalogue*, and descendants) emulates late-20th-century media: VHS, public-access TV, emergency alert broadcasts, instructional and training videos, weather service bulletins.
- The engine is **institutional tone over impossible content**: a calm, bureaucratic, instructional voice describing something horrifying ("If your reflection is not synchronized with you, do not acknowledge it. Remain indoors until sunrise.").
- Emergency Alert conventions: header tones indicated in brackets, issuing agency, affected area, instruction, repetition, end-of-message tone. The horror comes when an instruction violates normal alert logic.
- Fragments, archive numbers, corrupted sections ([TAPE DAMAGED]), schedule listings, test cards. The viewer or reader becomes an investigator.
- Keep it deadpan. Screaming adjectives break the effect.

## SCP-style clinical reports

- Item number, object class, containment procedures, description, addenda, interview logs, redactions (█████, [REDACTED], [DATA EXPUNGED]).
- The voice is clinical, passive, precise; the horror leaks through procedure. Numbers and procedures are specific.
- Formatting (headings, bold labels) is correct here; the scanner's format checks are off under `--genre`.
- Original work only: don't reproduce existing SCP entries, and note the SCP Wiki's CC BY-SA license if the user plans to post derived work there.

## Scripts (screen, stage, audio drama)

- Sluglines (INT. GAS STATION - NIGHT), action lines in present tense, character cues, parentheticals used sparingly. Dialogue carries the story; no novelistic interiority.
- Audio drama: everything must be audible; avoid characters narrating what they see ("Look, a knife!").

## Flash and micro fiction

- Flash: usually under 1,000 words (markets vary: 500, 750, 1,000). Micro: under about 300; drabble: exactly 100.
- One moment, implied before and after. Title does work. The last line turns without explaining.
- AI flash tells: a tidy mini-arc with a moral, and three atmospheric sentences before anything happens.

## Second person

"You walk into the kitchen." Works for immersion, games, and some horror. Keep it consistent, and make "you" specific enough to feel like a person.
