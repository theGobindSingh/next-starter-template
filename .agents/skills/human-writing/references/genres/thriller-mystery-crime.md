# Thriller, mystery, and crime

Three related promises. Decide which is the spine.

| | Thriller | Mystery / crime | Heist / caper |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core question | Will the hero stop the villain in time? | Who did it, and will they be exposed? | Will they get away with it? |
| Core value (Story Grid) | life / death, safety | justice / injustice | success / failure, justice inverted |
| Core event | hero at the mercy of the villain | exposure of the criminal | the job, and its twist |
| Core emotion | excitement, suspense | intrigue, satisfaction | delight, suspense |

## Thriller

Conventions and obligatory moments (Story Grid):
- an inciting crime that points to a master villain;
- the villain's MacGuffin (what they want) revealed through clues;
- red herrings and false leads;
- a clock: a deadline that makes wasted time cost lives;
- a speech in praise of the villain (their brilliance or reach is worse than thought);
- the villain makes it personal; the hero becomes a target;
- **hero at the mercy of the villain** (core event), where the hero's gift comes out;
- often a false ending: the villain returns once more.

Craft:
- Momentum over atmosphere: short chapters, cliffhangers that are real turns, not fake-outs.
- The villain must be competent and have reasons. A cartoon villain lowers the stakes.
- The hero's initial strategy fails; they adapt.
- Information control: what the reader knows versus what the hero knows (see horror's three not-knowing layers; they apply here).

## Mystery

Fair play is the contract. The classic statements (Knox's "Decalogue", 1929; Van Dine's rules, 1928) are dated in parts, but their core holds:
- The culprit appears early.
- All clues the detective uses are shown to the reader.
- No unknown poisons, secret passages sprung at the end, identical twins without warning, or supernatural solutions (in a non-supernatural mystery).
- The detective doesn't commit the crime or solve it by accident or intuition alone.

Structure:
- Crime discovered early; stakes that matter to someone.
- Investigation: interviews, clues, red herrings, a second crime or escalation.
- The detective's wrong theory, then the realization.
- Exposure of the culprit, with the logic laid out briefly; justice served or pointedly not.

Subgenres:
- **Cozy:** amateur sleuth, small community, violence off-page, humor, found family, often a hobby or shop. No graphic gore or sex.
- **Procedural:** police, forensic, or legal method depicted accurately; team dynamics; paperwork and politics.
- **Hardboiled / noir:** cynical first-person voice, corrupt city, morally compromised protagonist, often no clean justice. Noir can end in doom.
- **Legal thriller / courtroom:** procedure matters; cross-examination as combat.
- **Domestic / psychological suspense:** unreliable narrators, marriages, secrets, twists that recontextualize.

## Crime (criminal protagonist)

Heist, caper, and criminal-POV stories invert justice: the reader roots for the plan. Obligatory: the crew or skill, the plan, what goes wrong, improvisation, the reveal of the real plan.

## Genre-specific AI tells

- **The detective explains everything in a closing monologue** that restates every clue, then a warm wrap-up where everyone reflects on justice.
- **Clues the reader never saw**, or a culprit introduced late: AI drafts often violate fair play because they don't plan.
- **Unmotivated villain** ("some people just want to watch the world burn") or a villain monologue that explains the plan for no reason.
- **Therapy-speak interrogations**; suspects who articulate their feelings perfectly.
- **Stock procedural color:** the grizzled detective with a drinking problem and a divorce, the tech who "enhances" footage, "we've got a match", the chief who wants results by Monday.
- **Generic menace:** "a chill ran down her spine as she realized the killer was closer than she thought".
- **Twist with nothing planted.** A twist must recontextualize clues already on the page.
- **Everyone is "Dr."** and the experts are Emily Chen and Marcus Reynolds.
- **Tidy justice** in noir, where the genre expects cost or compromise.
