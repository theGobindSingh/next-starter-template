# Horror

**Promise:** fear the reader feels, not fear the narrator reports. **Core value** (Story Grid): life versus damnation, a fate worse than death. **Core emotion:** fear, and relief when it breaks.

## 1. The palette

Tim Waggoner (*Writing in the Dark*, Bram Stoker Award 2021) treats horror as five distinct colors. Plan which ones each scene uses; AI drafts use only "atmosphere" and a final jump.

| Color | What it is | How to write it |
|---|---|---|
| **Dread** | an unknown threat building, getting closer | small wrong details, rising frequency, withheld information, time pressure |
| **Terror** | the emotional and intellectual reaction to a threat now understood, its future implications | the character reasons about what it means and what comes next |
| **Horror** | the immediate recoil at an awful realization | a short, exact image or fact; don't explain it |
| **Shock** | a malicious surprise to the character and ideally the reader | set up so it's surprising but, in hindsight, fair |
| **Disgust** | bodily revulsion | specific and brief; used for effect, not wallpaper |

Waggoner's other key lesson: the unknown is scarier than the known, and over-explanation weakens both fear and story. Horror comes from a violation of reality: something that should not be, is.

## 2. Dread and momentum

Christopher Golden (CrimeReads, July 2026) boils horror-thriller craft down to two words:

- **Dread** is created in the reader, not on the page. Use a judicious selection of detail: too much or too little dilutes it. Set the mood early (even with a flash-forward) so the reader is anxious while meeting the characters.
- **Layer three kinds of not-knowing:**
  1. the reader knows something the character doesn't (dread, even horror);
  2. the character knows something the reader doesn't (mystery, suspense);
  3. neither knows (the most insidious; terror of the unknown).
- **Momentum.** Dread makes you hold your breath; momentum keeps you out of breath. Build it through word choice, paragraph length, and spreading character examination across the story instead of front-loading backstory. Pare scenes; split them and put something more urgent between the halves; use a clock. Even when characters stand still, urgency remains.
- **Care first.** Readers fear for characters they care about. "Give your readers characters they can truly care about, and then make it hurt to care." Isolate the characters: take away the help they would otherwise get (without making them stupid).

## 3. Conventions and obligatory moments

Story Grid's horror model. Not every short piece hits all of them, but longer work that skips them feels wrong.

Conventions:
- Ordinary settings that ground the fantastic.
- Labyrinths: claustrophobic spaces that hide danger.
- The monster cannot be reasoned with.
- Perpetual discomfort: attacks come without a schedule.
- The monster's power is masked and revealed progressively.
- The reader feels the monster's power while empathizing with victims.

Obligatory moments:
- an inciting attack by the monster;
- a non-heroic protagonist thrown out of normal life, forced to fight for survival;
- a speech (or revelation) in praise of the monster: it is worse than we thought;
- a series of losses or kill-offs leaving the protagonist as the final victim;
- **victim at the mercy of the monster** (the core event), where the protagonist's courage or gift comes out;
- a **false ending**: two endings, the threat returning after apparent safety.

The false ending is a structural beat, not an excuse for the AI habit of stacking codas. One false ending, then a real one, then stop.

Story Grid's subgenres by the nature of the monster: **uncanny** (rational, explainable: a psychopath, an alien), **supernatural** (spirit world), **ambiguous** (never explained: *The Shining*). Ambiguous horror is where "don't explain the monster" matters most.

## 4. Subgenres

Pick one as the spine; blends are fine.

| Subgenre | Engine | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Psychological | the mind as threat; unreliable perception | "was it real?" ambiguity is earned by consistent clues, not a final "or was it?" |
| Supernatural / ghost / haunted house | rules of a haunting; place as character | vary from the creaking-door kit |
| Cosmic / weird | indifference of the universe; knowledge that harms | specific wrongness beats adjectives like eldritch, unspeakable, ancient |
| Folk | community, ritual, land, tradition | real-feeling customs; outsiders who miss the signs |
| Gothic | decaying houses, family secrets, repression | ornate voice is allowed if controlled |
| Body | transformation, disease, the body betraying you | precise physical detail; disgust used sparingly |
| Slasher / survival | a killer or environment, escalating losses | kill-off structure, a final survivor |
| Creature | a thing that hunts | mask its power, reveal in stages |
| Quiet horror (Charles L. Grant) | slow crescendo of dread, character, atmosphere | subtlety needs precision; too vague and nothing lands |
| Domestic / social | family, relationships, society as monster | the real-life texture is the horror |
| Techno / analog / found footage | media and documents as the medium | see `frames.md` |
| Rules horror | a list of rules for a job or place; the rules are wrong or incomplete | see `frames.md` |
| Extreme / splatterpunk | transgression, graphic violence | only when asked; stays within platform policy |

## 5. Quiet vs loud

Quiet horror: suffocating dread, character, implication, mundane settings, questions left open. Loud horror: visceral events, shocks, gore. Most good horror modulates: long quiet stretches that make the loud moment land. Gore level follows the user's request; default is implication over anatomy.

## 6. Rule overrides

- Fragments and one-line paragraphs are legitimate at peaks of fear. They must deliver a new beat ("The door was open." after we saw it locked), not restate. The scanner allows up to about 45% one-line paragraphs for horror before flagging.
- Withholding is the point; "lead with the point" does not apply.
- Repetition can work as incantation in folk and gothic horror, when chosen.
- Stricter than default: explaining the monster, spectral vocabulary, stock scare phrases, happy resolutions.

## 7. Horror-specific AI tells

- **Explaining the monster.** A final paragraph of lore: "It was an ancient entity, older than time itself, that fed on fear." Cut it. Leave the reader with the wrongness.
- **"Something ancient stirred"** and its family: ancient, eldritch, older than time, from beyond, an entity, the void, a presence.
- **Spectral word overload:** whispers, echoes, shadows, humming, flickering, liminal, cold spots, in nearly every paragraph.
- **The stock sting:** "And then I heard the knocking." "I haven't slept since." "If you're reading this, it's already too late." "I can still hear it."
- **The creaky kit:** the door creaked open, the floorboards groaned, the silence was deafening, the air grew cold, a figure in the shadows, the lights flickered.
- **Reported fear:** "A wave of terror washed over me," "I was more scared than I'd ever been." Show what the character does because of it.
- **Dream cop-out** or "it was all in my head" twist with nothing planted.
- **Uplift ending:** the survivor heals, moves on, finds peace, learns courage isn't the absence of fear, faces whatever lies ahead. Horror can end with survival, but survival costs something and the threat isn't neatly gone.
- **POV slip:** a third-person story ending with a first-person "And then I heard...".
- **Generic setting:** "the old house at the end of Maple Street". Give it a real street, a real smell, a real neighbor.
- **Creepypasta clichés:** red or bleeding eyes, haunted game .exe files, spin-offs of famous pastas, mentioning creepypastas inside the story, impossible technical feats.

## 8. Checklist

- [ ] Which palette colors does each major scene use? Is there dread before horror?
- [ ] Which not-knowing layers are in play?
- [ ] Is there a clock or pressure that keeps momentum?
- [ ] Is the monster's power revealed in stages, and left partly unexplained?
- [ ] Do I care about the character before the worst happens?
- [ ] One precise wrong detail beats three atmospheric ones: did I pick it?
- [ ] Ending: costly, unresolved, or with one false ending, and no lore dump, moral, or healing montage.
